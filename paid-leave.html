Common questions

For a plain-language comparison, read Paid Leave Oregon vs. Washington PFML, explained.

How the weekly benefit is figured

Both states replace a big share of lower pay and a smaller share of higher pay, then apply a cap.

  1. Oregon: your average weekly wage is your total wages in the base year divided by 52. You get 100% of it up to 65% of the state average weekly wage, plus 50% of anything above that.
  2. Washington: your average weekly wage is your two highest-paid quarters added together and divided by 26. You get 90% of it up to half the state average weekly wage, plus 50% of anything above that, rounded down to the dollar.
  3. The result can't be more than the state maximum or less than the minimum for the period your claim starts in.
Which 12 months count?

Usually the first four of the last five completed calendar quarters before your claim. If that doesn't make you eligible, both states can use the last four completed quarters instead. For a claim starting in October 2026, the usual base year is July 2025 through June 2026.

Is there a waiting week?

In Washington, the first 7 days of leave aren't paid, except leave to bond with a new child or for a military exigency. Oregon has no waiting week.

Is paid leave taxed?

The states don't take out taxes automatically. Ask the agency whether you can have federal tax withheld, and check IRS guidance or a tax preparer about how your benefits are taxed.

Does paid leave affect SSI or SNAP?

Yes. Paid leave benefits replace wages, so SSI and SNAP generally count them as income. Use the benefits calculator to see the effect.

Can I take leave a day at a time?

Yes. Oregon pays in one-work-day or one-work-week increments. Washington pays in proportion to the hours you take off compared with your usual workweek, with a 4-hour minimum. This page assumes full weeks.
